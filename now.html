From foot drop to a PhD

A single thread runs through my work — from fitting orthoses in a Tehran clinic to a doctorate in Edinburgh: helping people living with mobility challenges move through the world a little more freely.

● Where I am now PhD Researcher Queen Margaret University · Edinburgh Characterising foot drop

I began my journey as a qualified orthotist and prosthetist in Iran. Soon after completing my education, I started working in a clinic to gain hands-on experience, assessing patients and designing orthotic and prosthetic solutions tailored to their needs.

During this time, I worked closely with a wide range of patients, particularly those with neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis (MS), cerebral palsy (CP), and stroke. Among these groups, stroke patients had a particularly strong impact on me.

What fascinated me most was the relationship between orthotic devices and walking ability. I became deeply interested in motion-analysis laboratories and how orthoses could improve gait and mobility. This curiosity led me to shape my Master's research around three key elements: gait analysis, stroke, and orthotic intervention.

Finding the gap

Through my clinical experience and early research, I identified a significant gap. Many stroke survivors experience foot drop, which severely affects their daily lives despite retaining some ability to walk. Although devices such as traditional ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs) and elastic braces like Foot-Up were available in the Iranian market, there was limited clarity on which option worked best. The literature itself often showed conflicting results.

Driven by this gap, I designed my Master's project to explore the question further. I introduced a new orthotic brace — imported from the United States for the first time in Iran — and compared it with existing devices. My aim was to evaluate the immediate effects of three different orthotic interventions on individuals with foot drop following stroke.

The findings

This highlighted the importance of personalised treatment approaches, where the selection of an assistive device should be tailored to each person's unique condition, lifestyle, and needs.

Beyond the findings, the experience was invaluable for my development. I gained strong skills in motion-analysis software, data interpretation, and clinical research — skills that shaped my academic direction moving forward.

A continuation, not an ending

My journey did not end there. Two years later, I came across a PhD opportunity that closely aligned with the questions I had started to explore during my Master's. It felt like a continuation of my work — and I immediately knew it was the right path for me.

Today, my PhD research expands on this foundation. I am exploring foot drop more broadly, regardless of its underlying cause, aiming to better understand both its simplicity as a term and its complexity in real-life experience. My work focuses on:

  • Understanding the lived experiences of individuals with foot drop.
  • Engaging with the professionals involved in treatment and care.
  • Developing a decision-making framework for selecting appropriate assistive devices, tailored to each individual.

My ultimate goal is not only academic contribution but real-world impact. I want to support people living with long-term conditions by ensuring that solutions are grounded in their daily realities — not just in clinical or theoretical outcomes.
